# The Nuremberg Censure — Charter (Full Version)

Long-term version · last revised 22 November 2026

*This is the full charter the prize is working toward. Until it is adopted step by step, [Charter v1](charter-v1) governs.*

*Statutes modeled section-for-section on the Nobel Peace Prize, inverted: a dishonor conferred on leaders responsible for grave crimes.*

## At a glance: Nobel Peace Prize vs. Nuremberg Censure

Every rule below copies a Nobel Peace Prize rule and inverts its purpose. Where a straight copy would not work for a dishonor, the change is marked.

## Article 1 — Founding Declaration

The Nuremberg Censure is awarded each year to the person or persons who did the most to commit, order or knowingly enable grave crimes in one of six categories: atrocity, aggression, repression, ecocide, plunder, or cultural destruction.

It does what the Nuremberg tribunal did in 1945–46: it says that no rank or office places anyone above the law. It is a public record, not a punishment. It calls for no harm to anyone. Its only demand is that each honoree answer to a lawful court.

## Article 2 — Criteria

A candidate qualifies in one or more of six categories. Each one rests on an existing legal standard, so every award can be checked against recognized law.

| Category | Covers | Legal anchor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atrocity | Genocide, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, war crimes, use of chemical, biological or nuclear weapons | Rome Statute; Chemical and Biological Weapons Conventions |
| Aggression | Starting a war to conquer or annex another country | Crime of aggression, Rome Statute; the "crimes against peace" tried at Nuremberg |
| Repression | Turning the state against its own people: torture, enforced disappearances, political imprisonment, starving civilians, jailing or killing journalists, propaganda that incites violence | UN Convention Against Torture; Rome Statute (persecution, incitement to genocide) |
| Ecocide | Severe and either widespread or long-term environmental damage, done unlawfully or wantonly, knowing it was likely | 2021 definition by the Independent Expert Panel for the Legal Definition of Ecocide |
| Plunder | Looting a nation's wealth on a grand scale while its people go without | UN Convention Against Corruption |
| Cultural Destruction | Banning a language or faith, forced assimilation (including taking children from their families), destroying monuments, places of worship and archives | UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, Article 8; 1954 Hague Convention; ICC *Al Mahdi* judgment (2016) |

**Naming.** Each award is named after its category, for example *The Nuremberg Censure for Atrocity*. The document that comes with the medal is called *the Indictment*.

**Phased opening.** The Nobel started with five prizes and added Economics in 1968. Here, Atrocity opens first, in 2027. Each other category opens once an adviser with expertise in that field has joined.

**Excluded on purpose.** "Undermining democracy" is not a category, because it is too disputed to judge neutrally. A military coup that takes power by force counts under Repression.

A candidate must have held, at the time of the acts, the authority to order or stop them: a head of state or government, a minister, a commander, or the leader of an armed group or ruling body.

## Article 3 — Eligibility

1. **Who.** Individuals, or a ruling body acting together (a junta, a governing council, an armed group's command). This follows the Peace Prize, which can also go to organizations.
2. **Shared awards.** No more than three recipients in one year, as in the Nobel statutes.
3. **No posthumous awards.** A candidate must be alive when the prize is announced. If an honoree dies after the announcement, the award stands. This is the Nobel rule since 1974. The acts may date from any time, as long as the honoree has not yet faced a court for them. The Committee gives priority to acts that are ongoing or took place within the last five years.
4. **Withholding.** If no candidate meets the evidence standard (Article 10), no prize is awarded that year.
5. **Repeat awards.** A person may be honored again for new acts. *Change from the Nobel: repeat honorees get an added bar on their existing medal, not a second medal.*
6. **No withdrawal.** Once announced, an award cannot be revoked. The one exception is if the factual basis is shown to be false (Article 7).

## Article 4 — Qualified nominators

Only qualified nominators may submit a nomination. Each nomination must be sent in writing with supporting evidence. The categories mirror the Peace Prize's:

- Members of national assemblies and national governments, and current heads of state
- Judges and prosecutors, current and former, of the International Court of Justice, the International Criminal Court and UN-backed criminal tribunals
- University professors, emeriti and associate professors of history, social sciences, law, philosophy, environmental science and archaeology
- Directors of peace research institutes, institutes of international affairs and human rights institutes
- Chairs of UN commissions of inquiry and fact-finding missions, and UN special rapporteurs
- Current and former members of the Nuremberg Committee and its permanent advisers

A nomination does not mean the Committee endorses it. Committee members may add names up to the end of their first meeting after the deadline, as Nobel Committee members can.

## Article 5 — The Nuremberg Committee

- **Size and terms.** Five members, each serving a six-year term that can be renewed. This matches the Norwegian Nobel Committee.
- **Appointment.** Members are appointed by the board of the Nuremberg Censure Foundation. *Change: the Nobel Committee is chosen by a national parliament. Here no more than one member may come from any single country, and at least four continents must be represented, so no single nation's politics controls the choice.*
- **Qualifications.** Members should include at least one international criminal lawyer, one historian and one environmental or heritage specialist.
- **Secretariat.** A director runs the Nuremberg Institute, which is the counterpart of the Norwegian Nobel Institute. A member of the Committee acts as its secretary. Neither the director nor the founder ever attends deliberations.
- **Advisers.** Permanent advisers prepare a written evidence report on each shortlisted candidate. Outside experts may be asked for opinions.
- **Conflicts of interest.** A member steps aside on any candidate from their own country or a country they have worked for.
- **Protection.** The Foundation may keep members' identities confidential if publishing them would put them at risk. *Change: the Nobel Committee's members are public.*

## Article 6 — The annual cycle

The calendar follows the Peace Prize cycle month by month. Only the two public dates move, to Nuremberg anniversaries.

1. **September:** Invitations go to qualified nominators.
2. **31 January, midnight CET:** Nomination deadline. Late nominations roll over to the next year.
3. **February:** At its first meeting the Committee reviews the nominations and may add its own.
4. **March:** Shortlist chosen. Advisers begin evidence reports.
5. **March–August:** Advisers review the evidence, and the Committee votes by 31 August. Shortlisted candidates who are not chosen are never contacted.
6. **September:** The honoree receives written notice and has 30 days to respond (Article 9).
7. **1 October:** Announcement, on the anniversary of the 1946 Nuremberg judgment.
8. **20 November:** Ceremony, on the anniversary of the trial's opening in 1945.

## Article 7 — Deliberation, secrecy and appeals

- **Decisions.** The Committee aims for unanimity. If it cannot agree, a simple majority decides, as on the Nobel Committee.
- **Secrecy.** The names of nominees and nominators, and the deliberations, stay sealed for 50 years.
- **Published record.** *Change from the Nobel:* each award comes with its full public evidence dossier, so the factual basis can be checked even while deliberations stay sealed.
- **No appeal.** No appeal against an award, as in the Nobel statutes.
- **Correction.** The one exception: if a court or a credible investigation shows the factual basis to be false, the Committee must review the award and publish its finding.

**Selection method.** Every shortlisted candidate goes through three steps:

1. **Evidence gate.** A candidate goes forward only if the evidence meets the standard in Article 10: at least one independent formal finding. Evidence is pass or fail and is never traded off against how grave the acts were.
2. **Scoring.** Each member scores each remaining candidate from 1 to 5 on four equally weighted factors: *gravity* (how many people were harmed, and how severely); *responsibility* (whether they ordered the acts, or could have stopped them and did not); *impunity* (whether they have escaped accountability entirely); and *recency* (whether the acts are ongoing or recent).
3. **Discussion and vote.** The scores open the discussion; they do not decide it. Members then vote.

**Quorum.** At launch, all three advisers must take part. Once there is a full Committee, four of five. If a member steps aside for a conflict of interest and quorum is lost, the board appoints a temporary member for that decision only.

**Ties.** If a vote is tied, members discuss again and vote a second time. If it is still tied, the candidate with the stronger evidence is chosen. If the evidence is equal, no award is made in that category that year.

**Limits.** At most one honoree per category per year.

**What is published.** Individual scores stay sealed with the deliberations. The published dossier states which factors decided the award.

## Article 8 — The award

Each award has three parts, matching the Nobel set of medal, diploma and prize-amount document.

**The medal**

| Spec | Nobel Peace Prize medal | Nuremberg Censure medal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diameter | 66 mm | 66 mm |
| Metal | 18-carat gold, about 196 g | Bronze, blackened (oxidized) finish |
| Front | Portrait of Alfred Nobel by Gustav Vigeland | Portrait of Joachim von Ribbentrop in the dock, sculpted from public-domain US Army Signal Corps trial photographs (main likeness: Truman Library accession 72-924; dock setting: National Archives photo 238-NT-612), with "CONVICTED · 1 OCT 1946" |
| Back | Three men in a fraternal bond, with *Pro pace et fraternitate gentium* | The empty defendants' dock of Courtroom 600, with *Nemo supra legem* |
| Engraving | Laureate's name and year | Honoree's name, year and crime category |
| Maker | Mint of Norway, Kongsberg | To be chosen through a medallic-art commission |

**The Indictment.** A hand-lettered document in the style of a bill of indictment, the counterpart of the Nobel diploma. It gives the honoree's name, the category, and a one-sentence citation of the acts.

**The victims' appeal.** *Inverted:* the Indictment names one vetted victims' organization tied to the case, and the announcement asks the public to donate to it directly. The Foundation never handles the money. The organization must have no ties to any political party, government or armed group. It must meet the same eligibility rules and checks as a victims' grant (Article 11). When funds allow, the board may also make a victims' grant from the Foundation's own funds.

## Article 9 — Ceremony and right of response

- **Ceremony.** Held on 20 November. The Committee chair presents the award, as the Nobel Committee chair does in Oslo. The chair reads each citation aloud, and a survivor or witness speaks for the victims.
- **The empty chair.** In 2010, Liu Xiaobo's Nobel medal and diploma were placed on an empty chair because he was in prison. Here, each medal is placed on an empty chair in a replica of the dock. It stays there in escrow, displayed at the Institute.
- **Handover.** The medal is handed to the honoree only when they appear before a lawful court for the acts cited. It is then given to that court as part of the record.
- **Right of response.** This replaces the Nobel lecture. The honoree receives written notice after the vote and has 30 days to respond. The response is published in full and unedited at the announcement, alongside the citation. If it shows a factual error in the dossier, the Committee may correct or withhold the award before 1 October.

## Article 10 — Safeguards

The Nobel has no need for these rules. A dishonor prize does: its credibility, and its legal standing, depend on them.

1. **Evidence standard.** Every award must rest on at least one independent finding: an ICC arrest warrant or indictment, a UN commission of inquiry, a court judgment, or concurring investigations by at least two recognized human rights or environmental organizations. The full dossier is published with the award.
2. **Neutrality.** The criteria apply equally to allies and adversaries of any government, including the founders' own. The Committee publishes, every year, which regions and governments its nominees came from.
3. **Wording.** Citations state documented findings and attribute each to its source. They do not declare anyone guilty of a crime. Before each announcement, a media lawyer in each relevant jurisdiction reviews the citation; UK and EU defamation law are stricter than US law.
4. **No incitement.** No imagery, words or event suggests execution or violence. The Foundation condemns any threat made in the prize's name.
5. **Name and marks.** Check that the name does not conflict with the City of Nuremberg's International Human Rights Award and the International Nuremberg Principles Academy, then register a trademark before launch. Never use "Nobel" in the prize's name, logo or slogans: the Nobel Foundation enforces its trademark. The website states that the prize is not affiliated with the City of Nuremberg, and never uses the City's coat of arms or logos.
6. **Legal home.** A nonprofit foundation holds the endowment, the trademark and the Committee's indemnity insurance.

## Article 11 — Funding

1. **Principle.** The prize never accepts money that could buy influence over who is honored, or look like it does.
2. **Refused sources.** Governments and state agencies at every level; state-owned companies and sovereign wealth funds; political parties, campaigns, candidates and political action committees; elected officials, even when giving personally; lobbying firms; armed groups; and anyone who is a nominee, the subject of a dossier or a past honoree, or who is closely tied to one.
3. **Accepted sources.** Individuals, and private foundations that are not funded by a government. No money from companies at launch; the board may revisit this once it exists.
4. **Cap per donor.** Once annual income passes $20,000, no single donor may give more than 25% of a year's income.
5. **Disclosure.** Gifts of up to $1,000 a year from one donor may be anonymous. Above that, the donor's name is published. Where the fiscal sponsor's rules are stricter, they apply.
6. **Firewall.** Donors never see nominations or deliberations and get no advance notice of an award. There are no naming rights on the prize, a category or the medal. Gifts cannot be earmarked for a category, or for or against a candidate.
7. **Conflicts found later.** If a donor becomes tied to a nominee, the Foundation returns whatever part of the gift it has not spent and records this in the annual report.
8. **Founder.** The founder's own contributions are disclosed like anyone else's. The founder takes no salary at launch; any future pay is set by the board, not by the founder.
9. **Free professional help.** Legal work, design and other donated services are disclosed the same way as cash.
10. **Annual report.** Each year the Foundation publishes its income by type of source, its spending, and the names of donors above the disclosure limit.
11. **The victims' appeal.** Donations prompted by an award go straight to the named victims' organization and never pass through the Foundation (Article 8).

**Use of funds.** Money may pay for:

- **Legal costs:** review of citations before announcement, defense against lawsuits, and insurance.
- **The award:** medal sculpting, casting and engraving; producing the Indictment; storing medals in escrow.
- **Evidence work:** research support for dossiers, translation, and secure storage of records.
- **Running the Foundation:** website, email, security, state and IRS filings, and accounting.
- **Expenses of advisers and board members,** reimbursed at cost with receipts. Not fees.
- **By board decision, once affordable:** a ceremony, staff, and victims' grants.

Money may never pay for: a salary for the founder at launch, or any payment to advisers or board members beyond receipted expenses; payment for evidence or testimony; lobbying, or supporting or opposing any candidate for public office; legal action against honorees; or anything that benefits a nominee or anyone tied to one.

**Legal reserve.** 25% of every donation goes into a legal reserve until it reaches a target set by the board.

**Gifts restricted to a purpose.** Donors may restrict a gift to a purpose, such as the legal reserve or the medal. They may never restrict it to or against a candidate, a category or a particular organization.

**Budget.** A budget is published at the start of each year, and actual spending in the annual report.

**The victims' grant.** A grant from the Foundation's own funds to one vetted organization that serves the victims of the acts cited, for example through medical care, legal aid, trauma counseling, or documenting evidence for future prosecution. It is not compensation or reparations, and it is never paid to individuals.

1. **Organizations only.** Never individuals.
2. **The board decides,** after due diligence, and keeps full control and discretion over every grant. Advisers may suggest organizations but never choose one. Donors may not direct money to a particular organization.
3. **Eligibility.** A registered nonprofit with at least two years of operating history and financial statements. It must have no ties to any political party, government or armed group, nor to the Foundation's advisers, board or donors.
4. **US-registered charities only,** until the board, on legal advice, opens grants to organizations abroad.
5. **Checks before any grant:** screening against US sanctions lists, a country check, and legal sign-off for any country under sanctions.
6. **A written grant agreement** setting out the purpose, a report within 12 months, and repayment if the money is misused.
7. **Payment only by bank transfer** to the organization's own account.
8. **Published:** the recipient, the amount, the purpose and the report. If naming the organization would endanger its staff, its name is withheld publicly but disclosed to the board and the auditor.
9. **Only when affordable:** after the legal reserve is full and the year's costs are covered. A victims' grant is never promised in advance.
10. **Not before tax-exempt status.** No grant is made until the Foundation has 501(c)(3) status and a lawyer has reviewed these rules.
11. **The same eligibility rules and checks** apply to the organization named in each victims' appeal (Article 8).

## Article 12 — Governance

1. **Separation of powers.** The board runs the Foundation: money, staff, the website and legal matters. The Committee (at launch, the advisers) chooses honorees. No one serves on both.
2. **The board.** At launch, three directors: the founder and two independent members. Each serves a three-year term that can be renewed. New directors are chosen by a vote of the existing board.
3. **The founder.** Chairs the board and runs the Foundation day to day. The founder never votes on honorees, never attends deliberations and never sees them. The board can replace the founder like any other director, so the prize can outlive its founder.
4. **Removing advisers or Committee members.** By a two-thirds vote of the board, and only for misconduct, an undisclosed conflict of interest or breaking secrecy. No one may ever be removed for how they voted.
5. **Amending the charter.** An amendment needs a two-thirds vote of the board and a majority of the advisers or Committee. Changes made after the 31 January deadline take effect in the following cycle.
6. **Sealed records.** Nominations and deliberations are stored encrypted. Within five years of each cycle they are deposited with a university or national archive, sealed until the 50 years are up.
7. **If the prize shuts down.** Sealed records go to that archive, still sealed. Escrowed medals go to a museum or archive of international justice. Any remaining money goes to another nonprofit with similar aims.
8. **Growth.** The charter is built to grow as people join:
    - *Board:* the board may grow to five or seven directors by its own vote. Once it has five or more, a majority must have no family or business ties to the founder.
    - *Committee:* when five qualified people meeting Article 5's requirements are available, the advisers' panel becomes the full Nuremberg Committee. Article 5's rules on nationality and continents apply from that point.
    - *Categories:* each new category opens as set out in Article 2.
    - *Volunteers:* the board may set up working groups (for example research, legal, communications or translation). Working groups support the Foundation but never take part in selection.
